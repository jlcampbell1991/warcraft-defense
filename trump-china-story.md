# Trump, China, and the US National Security Establishment

The summer of 2024 was a tinderbox of competing narratives. On the one hand, the U.S. national‑security establishment — the intelligence agencies, the Pentagon, the State Department’s security bureaus — warned of an increasingly hostile China. Their reports spoke of cyber‑espionage, aggressive naval posturing in the South China Sea, and a technology race that could leave America trailing.

On the other hand, former President Donald Trump, ever the maverick, began to push a different agenda. In a series of rallies and televised interviews, he argued that the United States was *missing* an economic opportunity by letting fear dictate policy. “China is a great trading partner,” he declared, “and we’re not going to let the deep‑state scare you away from making deals that put American jobs back on the table.”

Trump’s stance was not just rhetorical. He reached out to Chinese business leaders, floated the idea of a new bilateral trade summit, and even hinted at lifting some tariffs that the administration had kept in place as leverage against Beijing’s “unfair practices.”

The national‑security community bristled. Former intelligence officials warned that any concession could be weaponized — that economic interdependence would give Beijing a foothold to influence U.S. policy from within. In closed‑door briefings, they warned the president that “trading with the enemy is a Trojan horse.”

Yet Trump persisted, positioning himself as the lone voice willing to break the stalemate. He framed the security establishment’s caution as a “political racket” designed to keep America poor and dependent on government handouts. “We’ll make a deal,” he said, “and we’ll make it great again.”

The United States found itself torn. Business leaders and some members of Congress saw the potential for expanded trade, lower consumer prices, and a chance to re‑assert American influence through economic ties. Meanwhile, the defense establishment warned that each concession could erode strategic advantages and embolden a rival that was already outpacing the U.S. in critical technologies.

In the end, the story was less about a single decision and more about a nation caught between two competing visions of its future: one that trusted the instincts of a security‑first bureaucracy, and another that believed bold economic engagement could reshape the balance of power. Whether Trump’s gamble would pay off or backfire remains a question that history will continue to debate.
