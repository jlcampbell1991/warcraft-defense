# The Blast Door Dilemma

The blast doors were removed in preparation for a new training exercise that promised to push the base’s defenses to their limits. Engineers, pressed for time and budget, suggested a temporary substitute: thick sheets of plywood, reinforced with a few metal brackets. The decision was recorded in a hurried memo, its tone optimistic, its language unaware of the danger lurking beneath the veneer of wood.

At dawn, the crew lifted the massive steel doors, their clang echoing through the concrete corridors. In their place, rows of plywood panels were slid into the frames, their grain exposed to the harsh fluorescent lights. The wood, though sturdy enough for a warehouse, was not designed to withstand the shockwave of a true blast. Yet the commanders, eager to see the exercise proceed, signed off without a second thought.

The first test was a controlled detonation of a low‑yield charge intended to simulate an incoming projectile. When the charge ignited, the air filled with a searing roar, and a pressure wave surged forward. The plywood shuddered, splintering like a fragile shield. One panel gave way entirely, sending a spray of splinters across the control room. The remaining doors creaked under the strain, their seams popping as the force tried to find an escape.

Within seconds, the base’s alarms blared, and the engineers scrambled to assess the damage. The once‑solid barrier was now a jagged mosaic of broken wood, offering no protection from the lingering shock. Sensors indicated that the blast’s energy had been funneled deeper into the facility, compromising secondary systems that had relied on the doors’ integrity.

When the dust settled, the base’s engineers gathered the broken pieces of plywood, each splinter a reminder of the decision made in haste. The lesson was stark and unambiguous: replacing steel with wood in a warzone invites disaster, and the consequences of that choice echo far beyond the moment of the blast.
